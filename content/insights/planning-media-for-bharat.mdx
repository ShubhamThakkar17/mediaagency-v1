Growth for many Indian brands now lies outside the eight largest cities. Yet many plans for tier 2 and tier 3 markets are metro plans with a smaller budget. They lean on the same English creative, the same premium digital screens and the same assumptions about how people travel and shop.

Bharat needs its own plan. Here is what our team has learned about building one.

## Language comes first

In smaller towns, the home language is the language of trust. A translated headline is not enough. Idiom, humour and even the choice of visual change from state to state.

Plan the language mix before the media mix. A campaign for four states may need four creative versions, and the budget should allow for that. It is money well spent, because a message that sounds local is believed.

## The channels that carry weight

Some media that play a supporting role in metros become the main act in Bharat.

- **Television.** Still the family screen. Regional general entertainment and news channels deliver reach at a low cost per contact, and being on TV gives a brand standing with consumers and dealers.
- **Regional print.** District editions of Hindi and regional language dailies are read closely and passed around. They suit announcements, offers and anything that needs detail.
- **Railways.** Trains and stations connect the towns that other media skip. A wrap on a long-distance train travels through dozens of them.
- **State transport buses.** They carry a message from the district town into the surrounding villages, and they cost little.
- **Hoardings and wall presence.** A few sites at the bus stand, the main market and the highway entry can cover a small town.

Media we would usually leave out include metro rail, airport and most digital OOH networks. The inventory is thin or absent, and the audience is elsewhere.

## Digital works, but differently

Smartphones are everywhere, and short video is watched heavily in small towns. Social video in the local language is an efficient way to add reach, especially among younger audiences.

Lead generation needs more care. Form fills can be casual, so cost per lead looks low and quality disappoints. We prefer click-to-call and click-to-message formats, quick call-backs in the local language and a simple way to reach the nearest dealer. Search volumes are smaller than in metros, so search is a supporting channel in most of these plans.

## Borrow trust

New brands face more caution in Bharat. Three things help.

1. **A familiar face.** A regional film or sports personality can lend credibility faster than any amount of airtime. Regional names often cost far less than national stars and mean more locally.
2. **The dealer.** The local retailer or dealer is the most trusted voice in the purchase. Shop boards, in-store material and dealer names in the advertising all count.
3. **On-ground presence.** Haats, melas and festival grounds put the product in people's hands. Media builds familiarity. A demonstration closes the gap.

## Cluster the towns

Do not buy town by town. Group districts into clusters that share a language, a trade centre and media. One regional channel, one or two newspaper editions and a railway corridor can often cover a whole cluster.

Then phase the rollout. Prove the plan in one cluster, read the sales and dealer feedback, and extend it to the next. This protects the budget and sharpens the mix each time.

## Mind the calendar

Demand in smaller towns follows harvests, festivals and the wedding season more closely than it follows the financial year. A burst timed to the weeks when households have money to spend will beat an even spread across the year. Regional festivals differ by state, so the calendar must be regional too.

## The short version

Media is cheaper in Bharat, but attention is not automatic. Speak the language. Use the media people live with. Earn trust through familiar faces and local dealers, and grow one cluster at a time.
